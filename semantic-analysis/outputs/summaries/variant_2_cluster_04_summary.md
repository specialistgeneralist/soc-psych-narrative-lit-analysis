**Proposed cluster label:** Narratives as ideological frameworks linking motivation to action

**Synthesis paragraph:** Across these excerpts, a consistent theoretical construct emerges: narratives are socially validated, ideologically structured meaning-making frameworks that connect individual psychological needs—particularly the need for personal significance—to collective belief systems and prescribed behavioral responses. They function simultaneously as interpretive lenses, motivational scaffolds, and moral justification systems. Narratives identify grievances, assign causal responsibility, delineate ingroup/outgroup boundaries, and specify legitimate courses of action. Their efficacy depends on validation by a reference group, and their content—whether prosocial or radical—shapes the nature of resulting behavior. They operate at both individual and collective levels, anchoring identity within broader intergroup and ideological contexts.

**Sub-themes:**
- Significance quest as the primary psychological driver narratives address, linking personal deficits or aspirations to collective ideological commitments
- Moral legitimation function: narratives render otherwise unacceptable actions—including violence—cognitively and socially acceptable through framing and justification
- Social validation mechanism: narrative adoption and efficacy are contingent on endorsement by a valued ingroup, embedding individual meaning-making within group dynamics
- Ingroup/outgroup structuring: narratives organize social reality through value-laden differentiation between self-identified groups and designated adversaries or culprits

**Optional note:** The excerpts draw predominantly from radicalization and extremism literatures, which may limit generalizability of the construct to other narrative contexts.
